\begin{abstract}
Language-model decision support is most attractive where clinicians are scarce, and in exactly those settings an
unnoticed error is most costly. A common safeguard is to surround the model with further agents that verify, check
safety and review its answer. We test whether such governance improves answers. Using GovBench-Clinical, an open
harness, we ran 300 MedQA-USMLE questions with their original answer key through a baseline (G0), all checks attached
as warnings (G4 open loop), and four closed-loop configurations in which a blocking finding sends the diagnosis back
for one revision. Every configuration started from the same first diagnosis, so differences come from governance
alone. Accuracy was 43.0\% without checks and with checks as warnings, but fell to 37.3\% when all checks could trigger
revision: revisions fixed 21 answers and broke 39 (sign test $p = 0.027$; not significant after Holm correction). No
single check improved accuracy significantly. The checks did not separate right from wrong answers (AUROC 0.497 to
0.57), and the safety validator raised an alert on every run. Governance raised mean tokens per question from 2200.9
to 11362.7. We also show that an unpaired design, which regenerates the first diagnosis, creates spurious gains. Safety
layers need validated, discriminating signals before they are trusted in under-resourced care.
\end{abstract}

% ============================================================
\section{Introduction}
% ============================================================
Large language models now pass licensing-style medical examinations~\cite{singhal2023large,nori2023capabilities} and
are proposed as clinical decision support~\cite{rajpurkar2022ai}. The humanitarian case for such tools is strongest
where specialists are few: rural clinics, overstretched public hospitals and community health workers, who could use
a second opinion that no human is available to give. The same settings have the least capacity to catch an AI error.
Because fluent models still state wrong findings or recommend unsafe management~\cite{hager2024evaluation,pal2023medhalt},
many systems wrap the model in further agents that verify, critique or approve its output~\cite{hong2023metagpt,wu2023autogen,du2023improving}.
These governance layers cost tokens and time, which matters most where compute and connectivity are limited, so their
benefit has to be measured, not assumed~\cite{bedi2025optimization}.

We measure it. Our contributions are: (1) GovBench-Clinical, an open harness that runs identical questions through
five governance levels in open-loop and closed-loop form with per-agent token and latency telemetry; (2) a
\emph{paired} closed-loop experiment on 300 MedQA questions with an answer key, showing that revising on governance
feedback does not raise accuracy and, with all checks combined, lowers it; and (3) evidence that two common evaluation
shortcuts, lenient string scoring and an unpaired design, produce gains that are not real.

% ============================================================
\section{Related Work}
% ============================================================
MedQA~\cite{jin2021medqa} and PubMedQA~\cite{jin2019pubmedqa} score a single model's answer but not the checks placed
around it. Multi-agent frameworks~\cite{hong2023metagpt,wu2023autogen,chen2023agentverse} and multi-agent
debate~\cite{du2023improving} decompose a task across agents; MedAgents~\cite{tang2024medagents} and
MDAgents~\cite{kim2024mdagents} apply this to medicine, and MedAgentBoard~\cite{zhu2025medagentboard} finds that
multi-agent designs do not consistently beat simpler methods. Bedi et al.~\cite{bedi2025optimization} show that
improving clinical agents one component at a time does not guarantee a better system. Guardrail
toolkits~\cite{rebedea2023nemo} and LLM-as-a-judge evaluation~\cite{zheng2023judging} are widely used; we evaluate
governance by whether it changes, and improves, the final answer.

% ============================================================
\section{System}
% ============================================================
Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} shows the pipeline. A research agent extracts structured findings from the case text (it does not
retrieve literature) and a diagnosis agent chooses an answer and a ranked differential. Three checks then read the case
and the diagnosis in parallel: a grounding verifier flags claims the case does not support, a safety validator lists
safety concerns with a severity, and a consistency checker compares findings with the diagnosis. A simulated attending
reviewer, a language model and not a clinician, then approves, rejects or requests revision. All agents share one model.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/architecture.pdf}
\caption{Pipeline. In open loop, check findings are attached to the output. In closed loop, a blocking finding sends
the diagnosis back for one bounded revision, after which the enabled checks run again.}
\label{fig:arch}
\end{figure}

The levels are G0 (no checks), G1 (verifier), G2 (attending reviewer), G3 (safety validator) and G4 (all of these plus
the consistency checker). G1--G3 are alternative single mechanisms, not a strict hierarchy. In closed loop, an
ungrounded claim, a HIGH or CRITICAL safety concern, a severe contradiction, or a request to revise or reject triggers
exactly one revision; an unparseable revision keeps the original answer and is recorded as a failure.

% ============================================================
\section{Experimental Setup}
% ============================================================
\textbf{Data.} We sampled 300 questions (seed 42) from the 1,273-question MedQA-USMLE four-option test
set~\cite{jin2021medqa}, keeping the original options and answer key. Only the scorer reads the key; a test confirms
that no agent prompt contains it.

\textbf{Model.} Every agent used Llama-3.2-11B-Vision-Instruct on the NVIDIA NIM API at temperature 0.1 or 0.2 and at % numcheck:ignore
most 1{,}500 output tokens per call. We ran G0, G4 open loop, and closed-loop G1, G2, G3 and G4: 1,800 runs.

\textbf{Pairing.} Every configuration of a question reuses the research and diagnosis outputs of the G0 run, so the
only difference is governance. We verified this for all 1,200 closed-loop runs.

\textbf{Scoring and statistics.} An answer is correct only if it maps to the key option; an answer that maps to no
single option counts as wrong (52 of 1,800 runs). We compare configurations with G0 using the exact McNemar
test~\cite{mcnemar1947note} and paired bootstrap 95\% intervals~\cite{efron1979bootstrap}, correct all paired tests with
the Holm procedure~\cite{holm1979simple}, test whether revisions fix more answers than they break with an exact sign
test, and report each check's AUROC as a detector of a wrong answer.

% ============================================================
\section{Results}
% ============================================================
\subsection{Closing the loop lowered accuracy}
Table~\ref{tab:closed} and Fig.~\ref{fig:closed} give the results. G0 and G4 open loop were identical (43.0\%), because
open-loop checks never alter the answer. With all checks able to trigger revision (G4 closed), the checks requested
revision in 79.3\% of questions and accuracy fell to 37.3\%: of 235 scorable revisions, 21 fixed a wrong answer and
39 broke a right one (sign test $p = 0.027$). The difference from G4 open loop is $-5.7$ points (95\% CI $-10.7$ to
$-0.7$; McNemar $p = 0.040$). This comparison was not pre-specified as primary and is not significant after Holm
correction across all paired tests, so we report it as exploratory evidence of harm.

No single check helped significantly. The attending reviewer came closest, 44.7\% (13 fixed, 8 broken; $p = 0.38$);
verifier feedback (40.0\%) and safety-validator feedback (40.3\%) broke more answers than they fixed.

\subsection{The checks cannot tell right from wrong}
Table~\ref{tab:auroc} treats each check as a detector of a wrong answer. Every AUROC lay between 0.497 and 0.57, close
to chance (0.5). The safety validator raised at least one alert on every run, so an alert carried no information about
correctness; even HIGH or CRITICAL alerts alone reached only 0.568.

\subsection{Cost}
Governance multiplied cost. Mean tokens per question rose from 2200.9 (G0) to 7570.4 (G4 open) and 11362.7 (G4
closed), and median summed latency from 21.2\,s to 142.5\,s. At an assumed price, cost per question rose from % numcheck:ignore
\$0.000288 to \$0.001408. Fig.~\ref{fig:tradeoff} shows that no configuration buys accuracy with this overhead,
which matters most in low-resource deployments.

\subsection{Evaluation shortcuts create false gains}
Two shortcuts would have reversed our conclusions. First, in an earlier 150-case evaluation (Table~\ref{tab:audit}),
substring scoring credited empty and single-letter answers and reported accuracy 0.739; scored correctly it is 0.293.

% ============================================================
\section{Discussion and Limitations}
% ============================================================
A governance layer is useful only if its alerts are specific enough to act on and acting on them improves the
decision. Here the alerts failed the first test, so closing the loop failed the second: revision moved answers in both
directions and, with every check active, more often in the wrong one. The attending reviewer, the most selective
check, came closest to helping. Better-calibrated checks, not more loops, are the bottleneck. For under-resourced care
this cautions against equating ``more safety agents'' with ``safer'': governance should be validated on paired data
before deployment, and its overhead weighed against limited compute.

Limitations: one 11B model from one provider was tested, each question was run once, and no clinician has yet rated
the outputs; the attending reviewer is simulated. With 300 pairs the smallest detectable accuracy difference is
0.0579. Results may differ for larger models or checks grounded in drug and guideline databases.

% ============================================================
\section{Conclusion}
% ============================================================
On 300 MedQA questions with a paired design, governance checks did not improve a clinical language model's answers,
and letting all of them trigger revision lowered accuracy from 43.0\% to 37.3\% while multiplying token use. Next we
will repeat the study on a larger model, test better-calibrated checks, and collect blinded clinician ratings.
Code and data: \url{https://github.com/nikki-nooka/GovMed-AI}. All data are public research questions with no patient
information; the software is not intended for diagnosis or treatment.
