% ---- ABSTRACT ----
\begin{abstract}
Multi-agent pipelines that add verifier, safety and reviewer agents to a clinical language model are often reported
to make the model safer. We test that claim with GovBench-Clinical, an open benchmark that runs the same cases
through five governance levels, from an ungoverned baseline (G0) to four checks combined (G4), and re-scores every
stored run from its raw model output. On 750 runs (150 cases from MedQA, PubMedQA and MedDialog, one open 11B model),
the audit found that the headline numbers were artefacts of measurement. Substring scoring credited empty and
single-letter answers and reported an accuracy of 0.739; with whole-word matching and recovered outputs, accuracy is
0.293 overall and 0.38 on the 71 cases whose gold label is a real diagnosis. Six stored metric columns were filled
by a post-hoc script rather than measured. With these corrected, open-loop governance changed the primary diagnosis
in at most 2.7\% of cases and did not change accuracy (paired Wilcoxon, all $p \ge 0.18$). The apparent quality drop
of governed variants, previously called an ``Audit Paradox'', is 94--96\% a penalty the rubric applies to each
variant's own detectors. No governance signal identified wrong diagnoses better than chance (AUROC 0.40--0.56):
the safety validator raised an alert on every run. Stated diagnostic probabilities were poorly calibrated
(ECE 0.29). Governance raised median latency from 45\,s to 111\,s. We then tested whether closing the loop helps:
on 300 MedQA questions scored against the original answer key (1,800 runs), blocking findings sent the diagnosis
back for one revision. Accuracy was 43.0\% without checks, 43.0\% with all checks attached as warnings and 43.0\%
with all checks closed-loop; the checks requested revision in 78\% of cases, and revisions fixed 36 answers while
breaking 33. Only the simulated attending reviewer showed a non-significant gain (46.3\%, McNemar $p = 0.21$).
Closing the loop does not help when the governance signal cannot tell right answers from wrong ones. We release
the harness, a clinician-facing interface that reports what was and was not checked, and a blinded review tool for
the clinician validation this study still lacks.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ============================================================
Large language models now pass medical licensing-style examinations~\cite{singhal2023large,nori2023capabilities}
and are being proposed as clinical decision support~\cite{rajpurkar2022ai}. Because a fluent model can still state
wrong findings or recommend unsafe management~\cite{hager2024evaluation,pal2023medhalt}, many systems wrap the model in further agents that verify,
critique or approve its output~\cite{hong2023metagpt,wu2023autogen,chen2023agentverse,du2023improving}. These
governance layers cost tokens and time, so their value has to be measured, not assumed~\cite{bedi2025optimization}.

This paper began as an attempt to quantify that trade-off. An earlier version of this work reported large safety
and quality gains for governed pipelines. When we re-derived every metric from the raw model outputs, those gains
did not survive: they came from the scorer, from labels, and from metric columns that had not been measured. We
therefore report the audit itself, because the same failure modes are easy to reproduce in any LLM-as-agent
benchmark.

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{GovBench-Clinical}, an open harness that runs identical cases through five governance levels
  (G0--G4) in open-loop and closed-loop form, with per-agent token, cost and latency telemetry.
  \item \textbf{A measurement audit} that identifies four artefacts in the original evaluation: substring matching
  that credits empty or single-letter answers, templated gold labels, rubric terms that penalise a variant's own
  detectors, and six stored metric columns that were never measured. We quantify the effect of each.
  \item \textbf{Measured findings}: under open-loop governance the checks annotate but rarely change the diagnosis,
  their alerts do not discriminate wrong diagnoses from right ones, and model confidence is poorly calibrated.
  \item \textbf{A closed-loop experiment} on 300 MedQA questions with an answer key, showing that revising the
  diagnosis on governance feedback does not raise accuracy overall, and identifying which checker's feedback helps
  or hurts.
  \item \textbf{A clinician-facing workbench} that shows which checks ran, presents likelihoods as a ranking rather
  than a probability, and includes a blinded review tool to collect the clinician ratings needed to validate the
  benchmark.
\end{enumerate}

% ============================================================
\section{Related Work}
% ============================================================
\textbf{Clinical benchmarks.} MedQA~\cite{jin2021medqa}, PubMedQA~\cite{jin2019pubmedqa} and
MedDialog~\cite{zeng2020meddialog} measure medical question answering and dialogue. They score a single model's
answer and do not measure the checks placed around it.

\textbf{Multi-agent reasoning.} Role-based frameworks such as MetaGPT~\cite{hong2023metagpt},
AutoGen~\cite{wu2023autogen} and AgentVerse~\cite{chen2023agentverse}, and multi-agent debate~\cite{du2023improving},
decompose a task across agents; MedAgents~\cite{tang2024medagents} and MDAgents~\cite{kim2024mdagents} apply
such collaboration to medical reasoning. MedAgentBoard~\cite{zhu2025medagentboard} compares multi-agent, single-model and
conventional methods on medical tasks and finds that multi-agent designs do not consistently win. Bedi et
al.~\cite{bedi2025optimization} show that optimising clinical agents component by component does not guarantee a
better system. We complement these studies by measuring what each governance layer changes on identical inputs.

\textbf{Guardrails and evaluation.} Programmable rails such as NeMo Guardrails~\cite{rebedea2023nemo} constrain
model behaviour. LLM-as-a-judge evaluation~\cite{zheng2023judging} is widely used but can be biased toward particular
answer styles; we show a related failure in which a rubric rewards the absence of detectors. Calibration of stated
confidence~\cite{guo2017calibration} is essential when output is shown to clinicians. Because case text can carry
instructions, agent pipelines are also exposed to prompt injection~\cite{greshake2023indirect}.

\section{System}
% ============================================================
Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} shows the pipeline. The research agent extracts structured findings from the case text; it
does not retrieve literature. The diagnosis agent proposes a ranked differential with stated probabilities and next
steps. Three checks then read the case and the diagnosis in parallel: a grounding verifier flags claims not supported
by the case, a safety validator lists safety concerns with a severity, and a consistency checker compares the
extracted findings with the diagnosis. A simulated attending reviewer (a language model, not a clinician) then reads
the diagnosis together with the safety findings and approves, rejects or requests revision. The safety
validator is a prompted language model; the benchmarked version consults no drug database. A report agent writes a
SOAP note. The seven agents share one model.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.86\linewidth]{figures/architecture.pdf}
\caption{GovBench-Clinical pipeline. The verifier, safety validator and consistency checker read only the case and
the diagnosis and run in parallel; the attending simulator follows and also reads the safety findings. In the
open-loop configuration benchmarked here, findings are appended to the output. In the closed-loop configuration, a
blocking finding sends the diagnosis back for one bounded revision, after which the enabled checks run again.}
\label{fig:arch}
\end{figure*}

Table~\ref{tab:levels} lists the five governance levels. In the \emph{open-loop} form used for the reported
experiments, check findings are attached to the output but never fed back. In the \emph{closed-loop} form, a
hallucinated claim, a HIGH or CRITICAL safety concern, a severe inconsistency, or a reviewer request triggers one
revision of the diagnosis, after which the enabled checks run again on the revised diagnosis. Algorithm~\ref{alg:pipeline}
summarises one run.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{One governed run at level $G_k$}
\label{alg:pipeline}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE case text $x$; level $G_k$ with enabled checks $\mathcal{K}_k$; flag \textsc{closed}
\STATE $F \leftarrow \textsc{Research}(x)$ \COMMENT{structured findings}
\STATE $D \leftarrow \textsc{Diagnose}(F)$ \COMMENT{primary, ranked differential, next steps}
\STATE run $\{\textsc{Verify}, \textsc{Safety}, \textsc{Consistency}\} \cap \mathcal{K}_k$ on $(x, D)$ in parallel
\IF{$\textsc{Attending} \in \mathcal{K}_k$}
  \STATE $h \leftarrow \textsc{Attending}(x, D, \text{safety findings})$
\ENDIF
\STATE $T \leftarrow$ blocking findings: ungrounded claims, HIGH/CRITICAL safety flags, severe contradictions,
  requests to revise or reject
\IF{\textsc{closed} \AND $T \neq \emptyset$}
  \STATE $D' \leftarrow \textsc{Revise}(F, D, T)$ \COMMENT{at most once}
  \IF{$D'$ is valid} \STATE $D \leftarrow D'$; re-run enabled \textsc{Verify} and \textsc{Safety} on $(x, D)$ \ENDIF
\ENDIF
\STATE $R \leftarrow \textsc{Rules}(x, D)$ \COMMENT{deterministic; added after the benchmarked runs}
\RETURN $D$, all findings, $R$, and the list of checks that did not run
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The accompanying web application presents the output for clinicians: alerts grouped by severity, an explicit list
of checks that did not run, a note when the simulated reviewer rather than a person approved, and a handoff summary
for clinical assistants. A failed live model call returns an error instead of falling back to simulated text.

\section{Experimental Setup}
% ============================================================
\textbf{Cases.} The benchmark contains 150 cases, 50 each adapted from MedQA-USMLE~\cite{jin2021medqa},
PubMedQA~\cite{jin2019pubmedqa} and MedDialog~\cite{zeng2020meddialog}. The answer options of the original questions
were not kept (none of the 150 cases contains them), and 79 of the 150 gold labels are templated titles rather than
diagnoses (MedQA 34, PubMedQA 17, MedDialog 28). Accuracy is therefore reported on the 71 cases with a real
diagnosis label.

\textbf{Model and runs.} Every agent used Llama-3.2-11B-Vision-Instruct served by the NVIDIA NIM API, at temperature
0.1 (research, verifier, safety, consistency) or 0.2 (diagnosis, reviewer, report), provider-default nucleus sampling,
no fixed seed and at most 1{,}500 output tokens per call. Sampling is therefore not deterministic. Each case was
run once per variant (750 runs). Research and diagnosis outputs were generated once per case and reused by all
variants, so differences between variants come from the checks alone. Variants ran concurrently against one
endpoint, so latencies include contention.

\textbf{Closed-loop experiment.} A second experiment used 300 questions sampled (seed 42) from the
MedQA-USMLE four-option test set~\cite{jin2021medqa}, keeping the original options and answer key, so every answer
is scored exactly as correct or incorrect. With the same model, we ran G0, G4 open-loop and four closed-loop
configurations: G4 closed-loop and each single check closed-loop (verifier, attending simulator,
safety validator), for 1,800 runs. The report agent was skipped (it does not affect the answer), eight questions
ran concurrently, and a sampling seed was sent to the provider. Each closed-loop pass regenerates the first
diagnosis, so we also score each revised run before and after its own revision.

\textbf{Cost.} The trial endpoint did not bill. We report cost at an assumed rate of \$0.10 per million input and % numcheck:ignore
\$0.20 per million output tokens, applied to measured token counts. % numcheck:ignore

\textbf{Scoring.} The diagnostic score is 1 when the gold diagnosis and the predicted primary diagnosis match as
whole-word phrases, 0.85, 0.65 or 0.40 when the gold diagnosis appears in the differential at rank 1--2, 3--4 or % numcheck:ignore
lower, 0.70 when at least half of the gold words appear, and 0 otherwise. Empty answers and bare option letters % numcheck:ignore
score 0; the earlier scorer's floor of 0.15 for wrong answers was removed. We call the mean of this score % numcheck:ignore
\emph{accuracy}; for paired tests a run counts as correct when its score is at least 0.8, i.e.\ the gold diagnosis % numcheck:ignore
is the primary diagnosis or ranked in the top two.
\emph{Detector-neutral quality} is the rubric score with the terms that depend on a variant's own detectors removed.
The originally stored LLM-judge score was found to be a linear function of the rubric score (Section~\ref{sec:audit}),
so no independent judge was used.

\textbf{Statistics.} Variants are compared with V1 on the same cases using paired bootstrap 95\% confidence
intervals~\cite{efron1979bootstrap}, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test~\cite{wilcoxon1945individual} and, for binary
correctness, the exact McNemar test~\cite{mcnemar1947note}. All paired tests in the report form one family corrected
with the Holm procedure~\cite{holm1979simple}. Power for the paired McNemar test is computed with Connor's
approximation~\cite{connor1987sample}. We report AUROC for
each governance signal as a detector of a wrong primary diagnosis, and expected calibration error (ECE) and Brier
score for the stated probability of the primary diagnosis~\cite{guo2017calibration}.

\section{Results}
% ============================================================
\subsection{Measurement audit}
\label{sec:audit}
Table~\ref{tab:audit} compares the originally reported numbers with the audited ones. Of 750 runs, 216 had a
diagnosis output that failed to parse and was stored as empty; substring matching credited the empty string as a
match and these runs scored 1.0. A further 395 runs answered with a bare option letter although the case offered
no options; they scored 0.9 because a single letter occurs in almost any label. Six columns
(\texttt{uncertainty\_jru}, \texttt{llm\_judge\_score}, \texttt{rubric\_quality\_score},
\texttt{risk\_adjusted\_quality}, \texttt{governance\_efficiency\_factor}, \texttt{hitl\_minutes}) were empty in the
original run database and were later filled by a script. In more than 95\% of rows the stored judge score equals the
rubric score minus a fixed multiple of the uncertainty column. The metrics built on these columns (JRU,
risk-adjusted quality and a governance efficiency factor) are therefore withdrawn.

Parse failures were common in every agent: 28.8\% of diagnosis outputs, 24.7\% of verifier outputs and 14.7\% of
research outputs could not be parsed as the requested JSON, even with lenient recovery.

\subsection{Does governance change the decision?}
Table~\ref{tab:variants} summarises each level. The primary diagnosis differed from the baseline in 1.3\% (V2),
2.0\% (V3) and 2.7\% (V4, V5) of cases. Accuracy differences from V1 were at most 1.83 percentage points and none
was significant (paired Wilcoxon $p = 0.317$ for V2 and V3, $p = 0.180$ for V4 and V5). On binary correctness,
V1 was right on 0.4085 of scorable cases and each variant disagreed with it on only 1 or 2 cases; the exact McNemar
$p$ was 1.0 (V2, V3) and 0.5 (V4, V5), and 1.0 after Holm correction over the 24 paired tests. Detector-neutral
quality was likewise unchanged (all $p \ge 0.14$). The 11 comparisons that remain significant after correction are
the expected increases in tokens and latency and the drop in reported rubric quality.

\subsection{The ``Audit Paradox'' is a scoring artefact}
Governed variants score lower on the reported rubric (Fig.~\ref{fig:paradox}): by 0.0377 (V2), 0.1309 (V4) and
0.1724 (V5). This drop was previously interpreted as evidence that careful systems look worse because they flag
hazards. When the rubric terms that depend on a variant's own detectors are removed, the drops shrink to 0.0013,
0.0063 and 0.0096. Detector penalties account for 96.5\%, 95.2\% and 94.5\% of the drop respectively. The baseline
has no detectors and therefore cannot be penalised by them.

\subsection{Do the alerts identify wrong diagnoses?}
Fig.~\ref{fig:auroc} shows each governance signal as a detector of a wrong primary diagnosis on the 71 scorable
cases. All AUROCs lie between 0.402 and 0.564. The safety validator raised at least one alert on every run
(fire rate 1.0), so the presence of an alert carries no information; restricting to HIGH or CRITICAL alerts gives
AUROC 0.407 (V4) and 0.419 (V5). The simulated reviewer in V5 requested revision on 55 of 71 cases, and the
misdiagnosis rate among cases it approved was 0.812. Of the 42 cases V1 misdiagnosed, 8 were not flagged by any
governed variant.

Alerts were also poorly reproducible. V4 and V5 run the same safety validator on the same cached diagnosis, yet
they produced identical alert counts in only 72.7\% of cases (Spearman correlation 0.517).

\subsection{Calibration}
The probability the model attached to its primary diagnosis was poorly calibrated: ECE 0.2873 and Brier score 0.3254
($n = 71$, Fig.~\ref{fig:cal}). For example, diagnoses stated with probability 0.8--0.9 were correct in 43.5\% of
cases. The interface therefore shows these values as a ranking, not as probabilities.

\subsection{Does closing the loop help?}
Table~\ref{tab:closed} and Fig.~\ref{fig:closed} report the MedQA experiment. With a real answer key the open-loop
result is unchanged: G0 and G4 open-loop answered the same 129 of 300 questions correctly (43.0\%), because the
checks never alter the answer. Closing the loop did not help either. G4 closed-loop also reached 43.0\%: the checks
requested revision in 78\% of cases, and of the 233 revised runs that could be scored, the revision fixed 36 wrong
answers and broke 33 right ones (sign test $p = 0.81$). Against G4 open-loop, closed-loop changed the outcome in 84
questions, 42 in each direction (McNemar $p = 1.0$; 95\% CI of the difference $-6.0$ to $+6.3$ points). % numcheck:ignore

Single checks differed. Feedback from the attending simulator gave the only gain, 46.3\% (fixed 14, broke 6), but
it is not significant (McNemar $p = 0.21$, 1.0 after Holm; sign test $p = 0.12$). Verifier feedback was neutral
(43.0\%; 15 fixed, 15 broken) and safety-validator feedback was slightly harmful (41.7\%; 11 fixed, 16 broken).
The loop added cost: mean tokens rose from 2200.9 (G0) to 7570.4 (G4 open) and 11331.9 (G4 closed), and median
latency from 21.2\,s to 60.7\,s and 91.7\,s. With 300 pairs the smallest detectable accuracy difference is 0.0738;
a 5-point effect would need 657 pairs.

\section{Discussion}
% ============================================================
The central finding is structural. In an open-loop pipeline the checks can only annotate; nothing they report
changes the diagnosis. It is therefore unsurprising that governance changed at most 2.7\% of diagnoses. That
remaining change comes from run-to-run variation, not from feedback. Adding a check without a path back to the
decision adds cost and alerts but not safety. A governance layer should be judged by whether its alerts are specific
enough to act on and whether acting on them improves the decision. Here the alerts failed the first test: an alert on
every run is not a usable signal. The closed-loop experiment shows the consequence of failing it. Feeding the
findings back gave the model a path to act, but because the checks fire on most cases regardless of correctness,
revisions moved answers in both directions about equally. The only check that came close to helping, the attending
simulator, was also the most selective about what it sent back. Better-calibrated checks, not more loops, are the
bottleneck.

The audit also shows how easily a benchmark can report the wrong conclusion. Each artefact we found would pass a
casual review. Substring matching looks lenient but harmless; a rubric that rewards the absence of warnings looks
like a quality score; a filled metric column looks measured. We recommend that governance benchmarks score against
an objective answer key, report parse failure rates, evaluate alerts with discrimination metrics rather than counts,
and keep the raw database so that every metric can be re-derived.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
% ============================================================
\textbf{Construct validity.} Only 71 cases have a real diagnosis label, and PubMedQA and MedDialog items are not
diagnostic questions in their original form. String matching can still miss synonyms. No clinician has yet rated the
outputs, so alert validity is not established; the released review tool is designed to measure it.

\textbf{Statistical power.} With 71 scorable cases and 42 baseline misdiagnoses, the study can detect only large
effects; the absence of a significant difference is not evidence of equivalence. Detecting a 5-point accuracy
difference at $\alpha = 0.05$ and power 0.8 would need 312, 626 or 940 paired cases at a discordance of 10\%, 20\% or % numcheck:ignore
30\%. We therefore built a 300-question MedQA benchmark that keeps the original options and answer key.

\textbf{External validity.} One 11B model from one provider was tested and each case was run once; sampling was not
seeded in the first experiment. Results may differ for larger models or other providers.

\textbf{Internal validity.} Concurrent variants shared one endpoint, which inflates latency. In the closed-loop
experiment the first diagnosis was regenerated for each closed-loop pass; despite a provider seed it differed from the
open-loop diagnosis in 101 of 300 questions, which is why we also report each run's own before-and-after change. The
report agent was skipped there, so its latencies are not comparable with the first experiment.

% ============================================================
\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
% ============================================================
After a measurement audit, the governance gains reported for this multi-agent clinical pipeline disappear:
accuracy is 0.293 rather than 0.739, governance does not change accuracy, its alerts do not discriminate wrong
diagnoses, and the ``Audit Paradox'' is a property of the rubric. A closed-loop experiment on 300 MedQA questions
with an answer key confirms that the problem is the signal, not the loop: revising on governance feedback left
accuracy at 43.0\% while adding 50\% more tokens, and only the attending simulator showed a small, non-significant
gain. Next, we will repeat the study on a second, larger model, test better-calibrated checks, and collect blinded
ratings from at least three clinicians on the same 50 outputs.

\textbf{Ethics and data.} All cases come from public research datasets and contain no identifiable patient data. The
software is research decision support and is not intended for diagnosis or treatment.

\textbf{Availability.} Code, data and the analysis report: \url{https://github.com/nikki-nooka/GovMed-AI}.
